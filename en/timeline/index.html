Our Timeline

Four decades of pioneering Amazonian science

Milestones

How we got here

  1. 1970–78

    The SLOSS debate

    Emergence of the scientific debate on the Theory of Island Biogeography and the applicability of the acronym "SLOSS" (Single Large Or Several Small reserves of equal area).

    Creation of the SUFRAMA Agricultural District (DAS) north of Manaus to supply agricultural and livestock products.

  2. 1979

    The project is born

    Thomas Lovejoy, with support from WWF, joined INPA and SUFRAMA to plan forest fragments on farms within the DAS.

    Creation of the "Minimum Critical Size of Ecosystems" Project, coordinated by Richard Bierregaard, with demarcations and prior monitoring before interventions.

    Registration of the initiative as a CNPq Research Group (RG), named the "Biological Dynamics of Forest Fragments" Research Group — one of the longest-running groups in the Amazon.

  3. 1979–84

    27 reserves before clearing

    Establishment of 27 forest reserves prior to the forest fragmentation process in the DAS.

  4. 1985

    A protected area

    Creation of the Federal Conservation Unit ARIE BDFFP.

  5. 1988

    A new name

    Name changed to Biological Dynamics of Forest Fragments Project (BDFFP) following an external evaluation.

  6. 1990

    INPA and the Smithsonian

    Incorporation of the BDFFP into INPA's Ecology Research Coordination through an agreement between MCT-INPA and the Smithsonian Institution.

  7. Today

    The continuing legacy

    The BDFFP continues as a living school, developing diverse projects and courses across the Amazon region.

Science for Amazon conservation

Since 1979, we have studied how fragmentation of the Amazon rainforest affects biodiversity. We turn science into conservation through long-term research, training new scientists and a continuous presence in the region.
